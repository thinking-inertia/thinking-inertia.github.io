Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly ship with explicit “thinking modes,” yet their counterpart, “no-think,” has received far less attention. In this work, we study LLMs’ “no-think” behavior along two axes.

a. How to measure “no-thinking”? Prior work typically defines “no-thinking” through proxies such as a disabled thinking mode or the absence of long traces. These proxies are unreliable: disabled thinking modes still emit extended reasoning, or long traces often contain uninformative filler rather than genuine inference. We instead normalize any LLM response into pre-answer trace and final answer, and evaluate it at three levels: (i) an Empty-Thinking Ratio (ETR) for strict answer-only compliance; (ii) an instruction-aware Question–Pre-answer Relevance (QRel.) score to measure similarity between the question and pre-answer trace; and (iii) an additional LLM-as-judge Explicit Inference Rate (EIR) for visible explicit inference. Together, these levels separate answer-only output, relevant but non-inferential text, and explicit inference. Human studies demonstrate the effectiveness of these metrics.

b. How does no-thinking vary across tasks and models? We evaluate six prompting intervention settings on six widely used LLMs, both open-source and commercial, across three question types: Boolean, multiple-choice, and open-ended. We find that explicit no-think controls cannot reliably eliminate visible explicit inference; models instead exhibit “Thinking Inertia”: visible explicit inference persists even under strict no-thinking controls and becomes progressively more prevalent as the answer space opens. Accuracy remains relatively stable on Boolean and multiple-choice tasks, whereas open-ended tasks expose a trade-off between answer-only compliance and task accuracy. Finally, rewriting the same questions across answer spaces shows that supplying candidate answers makes answer-only responses substantially easier to produce. Taken together, these findings establish no-thinking as a non-trivial capability of LLMs: stopping explicit reasoning cannot be assumed from model settings or instructions alone, and deserves systematic evaluation alongside reasoning ability itself.

The problem in one sentence. A model can be told not to think, omit a <think> block, or produce a short response—and still expose a compact, task-specific inferential step.

A response is more than a thinking switch

We treat no-thinking as an observable response property. An intervention wraps the question, the model emits a response, and the response is decomposed into the visible pre-answer text T and the final answer A. The same final answer can be preceded by empty text, generic filler, relevant assertions, or an explicit inferential step.

Figure 2. Response-centric measurement framework. The top shows the inference process and the decomposition into T and A; the bottom shows six response cases sharing the same question and final answer but differing in length, relevance, and inferential content.

From answer-only format to explicit inference

The evaluation hierarchy makes each claim narrower and more testable. ETR checks the strict boundary, QRel. supplies an instruction-aware relevance signal, and EIR directly classifies visible inference. QRel. guides interpretation but is not itself evidence of reasoning.

ETR — Empty-Thinking Ratio. Strict answer-only compliance: the share of outputs with an empty visible pre-answer field T.

The Level 3 rubric separates generic/off-topic, paraphrase-only, relevant non-inferential, explicit reasoning, and unclear text; empty T is no visible inference by definition.

What the experiments reveal

1. Answer space creates a staircase

Across native no-thinking and stricter no-thinking controls, the visible-inference ordering is consistent:

Bool<MCQ<Openmore answer freedom, more visible inference

The pattern is not a consequence of a single proxy: ETR, QRel., and EIR show complementary aspects of the same response behavior.

Results overview. The current three-level view pairs strict compliance, visible explicit inference, instruction-aware relevance, and pre-answer length across answer spaces and intervention modes.

2. Stricter controls reduce, but do not erase, visible inference

Within every answer space, the mode relation is M5 < M2. Stronger answer-only constraints suppress the visible signal, while the open-ended regime remains the most resistant—the response-level signature of thinking inertia.

M5<M2the relation holds for Bool, MCQ, and Open
3. Accuracy and compliance are separate axes

A cleaner answer-only format does not automatically mean a more capable answer. We therefore read accuracy on A alongside ETR, QRel., and EIR rather than treating any single output statistic as a complete definition of no-thinking.

4. Matched rewrites isolate the answer interface

When the same underlying question is rewritten as Boolean, multiple-choice, or open-ended, the interface changes while task difficulty is held as closely as possible. Short targets alone do not explain the pattern: matched lengths can still differ sharply in question relevance.

Checks that keep the interpretation narrow

The main conclusions do not rest on QRel. alone. Alternative encoders test the relevance signal, Claude Opus 4.8 provides an independent-family replication of the Level 3 judge, and a blinded five-annotator study checks the metric hierarchy and its central answer-space and mode comparisons. These checks support claims about visible response behavior, not latent cognition.

Reading guide. ETR tells us whether the response reaches the answer-only boundary; QRel. tells us whether residual text is question-relevant; EIR tells us whether that text contains an explicit inferential step.
